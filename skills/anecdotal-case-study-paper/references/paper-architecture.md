# Paper architecture

## Purpose

Use this reference when the user wants a complete formal paper rather than a short joke, social post, or slide deck. The structure should make the paper legible to a reader who expects a real case study, while preserving the absurd premise as an explicitly labeled hypothesis.

## Title and front matter

Use a serious, bounded main title. Put the humorous or dramatic idea in a subtitle.

Preferred pattern:

> Main topic + study design
>
> Subtitle: On the explanatory boundary of the "[hypothesis]" hypothesis

Avoid:

- a title that asserts a fictional identity as fact;
- a title that reveals a real person's private information;
- a title built only from a punchline;
- an English title that is a literal machine translation.

Front matter should include:

- Chinese title and English title;
- author or research-group label;
- affiliation or "independent / unofficial research group";
- abstract with data source, method, main finding, and uncertainty;
- keywords;
- classification or document metadata only when useful;
- a note that the paper is a preprint, satire, or entertainment artifact when appropriate.

## Abstract

The abstract should answer five questions in order:

1. What was observed?
2. How was it observed?
3. What quantitative or comparative pattern appeared?
4. Which explanations were tested?
5. What is the strongest defensible conclusion, including uncertainty?

Do not make the abstract funnier than the evidence. The contrast between neutral language and an absurd subject is the joke.

## Introduction

Include:

- the everyday puzzle;
- why the puzzle appears repeatedly;
- the user's preliminary hypothesis;
- the research questions;
- the paper's contribution;
- a scope boundary separating observation from ontology.

Useful research-question shape:

- RQ1: How large is the mismatch between reported and external time?
- RQ2: Can one fixed conversion factor explain every case?
- RQ3: Which rival explanation has the greatest explanatory scope and testability?

## Literature and framework

Use a small number of real, relevant sources. Typical clusters:

- subjective time and time perspective;
- procrastination and deadlines;
- sleep inertia or circadian timing;
- science communication and cultural metaphors;
- the fictional corpus only as a cultural reference.

Build an analytical framework with distinct levels, for example:

- task level: duration, queue, task switching;
- person level: habits, sleep, attention, time perspective;
- narrative level: why the fictional explanation is attractive.

Do not use a real scientific citation to imply that a fictional mechanism has empirical support.

## Methods

Describe:

- participant or case;
- design, often `n=1`, opportunistic natural observation;
- observation setting and sampling;
- coding fields;
- data sources and triangulation;
- evidence grading;
- missing-data policy;
- ethics and privacy.

Operationalize ambiguous phrases. For example:

- "a few seconds" needs an assumed range;
- "about half an hour" needs a named speaker and reference frame;
- "one year of preparation" needs a reference assumption.

State explicitly when the paper uses descriptive ratios rather than inferential statistics.

## Results

Use case subsections. For each case:

1. what happened;
2. what was reported;
3. what was externally observed;
4. what can be calculated;
5. what cannot be concluded;
6. which figure or table supports the paragraph.

Keep contradictory evidence. A negative case or failed replication is often more useful than another dramatic anecdote.

## Model and hypothesis testing

A descriptive model is acceptable when labeled as descriptive. Separate:

- equation as bookkeeping device;
- equation as physical theory;
- equation as joke.

Include rival explanations and a falsification table with columns such as:

- hypothesis;
- testable prediction;
- minimum data;
- current status.

A good paper states in advance what evidence would weaken or reject its preferred story.

## Discussion

Discuss:

- explanatory reach and boundary;
- narrative efficiency versus falsifiability;
- practical implications;
- what would change the authors' minds;
- why the serious form is useful for examining a trivial or absurd claim.

## Limitations

At minimum consider:

- sample size;
- measurement error;
- construct validity;
- retrospective memory;
- third-party testimony;
- sampling bias;
- observer bias;
- demand characteristics;
- missing outcome variables;
- external validity;
- privacy and consent.

## Conclusion

Return to the strongest defensible claim. Do not upgrade "a mismatch exists" into "a supernatural mechanism exists." A good ending can be funny, but it should still be logically bounded.

## Tables and appendices

Use numbered three-line tables in formal layouts. Common appendices:

- minimal replication protocol;
- observation-log coding sheet;
- evidence ledger;
- glossary of operational definitions.

Appendices should help a future reader reproduce the observation, not add new unverified claims.
